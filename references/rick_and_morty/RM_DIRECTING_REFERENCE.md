# Rick and Morty directing grammar — reference for court-case reenactment channel

Scope: directing/editing/timing techniques only. No character, design, catchphrase, or asset
copying. Grammar transfer target: Korean 2D SVG-puppet court-case Shorts (9:16, ~25–30s) and
long-form (16:9, 8–10min).

## 1. Production pipeline (what actually happens, per sources)

- Each episode starts with a **"launch" meeting** (writers, showrunners, producers, designers,
  storyboard artists) to break the script scene-by-scene, then sequences are handed to individual
  storyboard artists by strength (action vs. dialogue vs. backgrounds). Season 1 used 8 storyboard
  artists across 4 teams; by Season 4 this grew to 20 artists across 5 teams. Storyboarding takes
  roughly 1–1.5 months per episode; full launch-to-air is **9–12 months**.
  Source: director Erica Hayes interview — https://www.inverse.com/article/60708-rick-and-morty-erica-hayes-how-to-make-an-episode-future-50
- The editor assembles storyboard panels into a rough **animatic** ("a little storyboard movie")
  before showrunner review — i.e., editing-for-rhythm happens *before* any animation is drawn.
  Same source as above.
- Animation is produced by **Bardel Entertainment** (Vancouver) using Toon Boom (Harmony /
  Storyboard Pro) and other 2D tools; the show is described as a **vector-based 2D "puppet" show**
  — rigged, reusable character parts rather than frame-by-frame hand-drawn redraws, which is an
  economic/production-speed choice, not a stylistic accident. This is the same category of rig
  our SVG-puppet pipeline uses, which is directly relevant.
  Source: animation critique video essay by Canipa, covered by Cartoon Brew —
  https://www.cartoonbrew.com/video-essay/video-essay-why-the-animation-on-rick-and-morty-needs-to-change-208756.html
  (Note: this is a critical/analytical video essay, not a studio statement — treat its claims as
  one informed outsider's reading, not confirmed production doctrine.)
- Animation director Nathan Litz (Bardel) describes the studio's goal as making the show "look
  like one person animated the whole show" through enforced style rules across a ~50-person
  crew — consistency is a deliberate discipline, not an emergent property.
  Source: https://bardel.ca/spotlight-emmy-winning-rick-and-morty-director-nathan-litz/
  (This interview did not yield specifics on squash-and-stretch or rigging mechanics — flagged
  as uncertain/unavailable rather than invented.)

## 2. Dialogue, timing, and "retroscripting"

- The show is associated with **"retroscripting"**: actors record with only a loose outline and
  improvise, and animation/timing is built around the ad-libbed performance rather than a locked
  script. This is widely reported but I could not find a single authoritative primary-source
  definition; treat the term as reasonably well-attested fan/press shorthand, not studio-official
  terminology. Source (general description): https://www.inverse.com/article/4879-cartoon-network-s-rick-and-morty-goes-from-weird-to-weirder-to-must-watch
- **This has measurably declined over the seasons.** Current voice actors (Ian Cardoni, Harry
  Belden) report scripts are now tightly written, with improvisation limited mostly to stammers,
  burps, and line delivery — not content. Actors do **not** record together, which removes
  real-time back-and-forth. Source: https://tvline.com/2102118/how-much-rick-and-morty-improvised
- Showrunner framing: "Creative work is really, truly facilitated by limitation" (Dan Harmon),
  and the writers deliberately hand scenes to animators with latitude ("Go crazy with this") to
  layer in visual gags during fight/action sequences beyond what's scripted.
  Source: https://www.slashfilm.com/974261/rick-and-mortys-dan-harmon-and-ep-scott-marder-on-sustaining-the-shows-quality-perfecting-the-comedy-more-interview/
- No interview found that states explicit frame-counts for holds, reaction-shot length, or
  timing rules — these appear to be craft intuition exercised shot-by-shot by storyboard
  artists/directors, not a documented formula. Flagged as **unavailable**, not zero.

## 3. Measurable data — explicitly checked, mostly absent

- **No published average-shot-length (ASL) or cuts-per-minute figures exist for Rick and Morty**
  specifically, searched directly and via general ASL/cutting-rate literature (Nick Redfern's
  "Analysing Motion Picture Cutting Rates," https://widescreenjournal.org/analysing-motion-picture-cutting-rates-by-nick-redfern,
  and general ASL explainer https://tools.wbez.org/average-shot-length). Any specific number
  attributed to R&M's cutting pace online is not independently verifiable from a credible source
  — do not use invented numbers.
- For contrast, **South Park's** production is well-documented as extremely fast-turnaround
  (script-to-air in about six days), which forces a leaner, more montage-driven visual grammar
  than R&M's much longer (9–12 month) pipeline. Source: https://looper.com/1092821/south-parks-breakneck-production-is-managed-down-to-the-minute
  — useful only as a contrast point per the brief, not a technique to copy wholesale.

## 4. Named techniques actually attributable to the sources above

Confirmed-in-sources:
- Pre-animation **editing pass on the animatic** for pacing/staging before a single frame is
  finished-animated (Erica Hayes / Inverse).
- Director sign-off criterion explicitly named: checking for "no weird cuts or strange staging" —
  i.e., visual-flow continuity is a named QA gate, not just an aesthetic preference (same source).
- Reused, rigged ("puppet") character parts as the base animation unit (Canipa/Cartoon Brew).
- Enforced studio-wide style consistency despite a large, multi-team crew (Bardel/Nathan Litz).
- Writers deliberately under-specifying physical comedy beats so storyboard/animation teams add
  gags (Harmon/Marder, Slashfilm).

Widely discussed in general animation/comedy craft literature but **not confirmed specifically
for Rick and Morty** by any source found here (treat as general technique, not R&M-sourced fact):
smash cuts, snap/crash zooms, deadpan holds, cold opens, montage sequences, stylized gore. These
are genre-standard adult-animation/sketch-comedy tools, well documented generically (e.g., generic
snap-zoom definition: a near-instant focal-length change over roughly 2–4 frames, used for comedic
jolt — https://morphic.com/ar/ai-glossary/snap-zoom), but I found no citable R&M-specific
breakdown naming these by shot/episode. I'm marking these as **inferred from general adult-
animation comedy convention**, not verified R&M documentation, per the no-invented-numbers rule.

## 6. Practical rules for our SVG-puppet courtroom/quiz format

Each rule states the technique, an approximate timing unit, and whether it is (a) directly
sourced from R&M production material above, or (b) general adult-animation/comedy-editing
convention applied to our format (marked `[general]`). No rule invents a frame-count claimed to
be R&M's own.

1. **Cut the animatic for rhythm before polishing art.** Storyboard the whole Short/episode as a
   rough edit first; fix pacing dead air there, not after assets are finished. `[sourced — Hayes]`
2. **QA gate: no "weird cuts or strange staging."** Before export, watch once only for jarring
   geography/eyeline breaks between shots. `[sourced — Hayes]`
3. **Under-write the reaction, over-direct the gag.** Script only the legal content; let the
   animator add one visual beat (gavel flinch, juror side-eye) per key line. `[sourced — Harmon/Marder]`
4. **One consistent puppet-rig "voice."** Lock proportions/expression range across all reused
   character rigs so a judge, defendant, and narrator all read as one show's house style, even
   across different Shorts. `[sourced — Litz/Bardel]`
5. **Reuse rig parts aggressively, but vary timing, not shape.** Since the rig is the same across
   episodes, get dynamism from hold-length and cut-timing variation, not new geometry every scene.
   `[sourced — Canipa/Cartoon Brew, re: economics of puppet rigs]`
6. **Smash-cut the courtroom's dead beats.** Cut straight from a question to the answer's
   consequence, skipping the walk/pause; target under ~0.5–1s of "nothing happening" per cut.
   `[general — adult-animation comedy convention]`
7. **Snap-zoom on the verdict/sentence reveal.** A ~2–4 frame push into the judge's face or the
   defendant's eyes sells the stakes beat. `[general — standard snap-zoom technique]`
8. **Deadpan hold after the punchline/verdict**, roughly 0.5–1s of total stillness before cutting
   away, so the gravity/absurdity of a ruling registers. `[general]`
9. **Never let a static dialogue scene sit on one framing for more than ~2–3s.** Rotate
   over-the-shoulder / reaction / wide every few seconds even if no new blocking exists — swap
   angle, not geography. `[general, consistent with Hayes' "no strange staging" QA but angle-cycling itself is general practice]`
10. **Cold open on the crime/charge, not on setup.** Start Shorts and long-form cold on the
    incident or the charge being read; deliver context as flashback/cutaway after the hook.
    `[general — standard adult-animation cold-open convention]`
11. **Montage the procedural grind.** Compress investigation/trial-prep into a quick-cut montage
    (3–6 shots, ~0.5–1s each) rather than narrating it in real time. `[general]`
12. **Stylize consequence, don't dwell on it.** For violent/criminal content, cut away at the
    impact beat and show the aftermath/reaction rather than lingering — keeps tone punchy without
    becoming gratuitous. `[general, informed by R&M's puppet-rig economy favoring suggestion over detail]`
13. **Act-break cliffhanger before each ad/segment break in long-form.** End on an unresolved
    question (does the court buy the alibi?) at the ~2–3 minute mark in an 8–10 min piece.
    `[general]`
14. **A/B/C quiz choice: snap-zoom each option in sequence**, ~0.3–0.5s push per option, with a
    distinct SFX/pose per option so viewers feel three discrete "beats" rather than one static
    list. `[general, applying snap-zoom technique to a UI moment]`
15. **Hold on viewer's choice point longer than any other shot in the piece** (your one deliberate
    "long hold") so the quiz moment reads as the climax, not a pause — direct inversion of the
    deadpan-hold technique above, now used for suspense instead of comedy. `[general]`
16. **Vary shot scale every 2–4 cuts even within one conversation** — wide → medium → close → OTS
    rotation keeps a two-person courtroom exchange from feeling like a static VN-style dialogue
    box. `[general]`
17. **Give every reused rig a "tell" micro-gesture** (e.g., the judge always adjusts glasses before
    a ruling) so repeated assets still feel alive and foreshadow beats. `[general, in spirit of Litz's "one consistent house style" discipline]`
18. **Front-load the absurd/shocking detail of the case in the first 2–3 seconds of a Short** —
    cold-open convention #10 applied specifically to the 25–30s format where there is no time for
    a slow build. `[general]`
19. **Use a quick 3-shot "evidence montage" instead of a single evidence-reveal shot** to turn
    exposition into rhythm. `[general]`
20. **Keep comedic/ironic beats (if any) under-written in dialogue and over-delivered in staging** —
    mirrors R&M's writers-trust-animators approach: write the legal fact flatly, let blocking and
    one exaggerated reaction-pose carry the irony. `[sourced — Harmon/Marder]`
21. **Treat the storyboard/animatic cut as the real editing pass**, not the final render — for our
    pipeline this means locking shot order and timing in a rough Remotion pass before final puppet
    animation polish, matching R&M's animatic-first workflow. `[sourced — Hayes]`
22. **Do not claim a specific cuts-per-minute target as "the Rick and Morty number"** — none is
    published; set our own target empirically from what plays well, not from a borrowed statistic.
    `[explicit caution, not a technique]`
