No Wikipedia, fan sites or "who would win" blogs are used as sources. Sites in that category that came up in searches (a-z-animals, animalmatchup, wildfact, Quora, Reddit, Times of India "who would win" photostory) are excluded.

Conversion constants: 1 kg = 2.2046 lb; 1 mph = 1.609 km/h; 1 in = 2.54 cm; 1 N = 0.2248 lbf; 1 US fl oz = 29.57 mL; 1 US (short) ton = 2,000 lb.

### Encounter behaviour (real world)

- **Polar bears do eat walruses, but walruses are minor prey; mostly adult male bears take them.** Derocher, Lunn & Stirling 2004, "Polar bears in a warming climate", *Integrative and Comparative Biology* 44:163–176 (PDF copy): https://naturalresourcespolicy.org/docs/polar-bears-in-warming-climate.pdf — verified 2026-11-13.
  - "Walrus are a relatively minor prey species of polar bears in most areas"
  - "Given the large size of even subadult and young walrus, it is likely that only adult male polar bears would be able to exploit walrus as prey"
  - "walrus are aware of the danger represented by polar bears and are capable of threatening and possibly killing polar bears themselves"
- **Calves and weakened walruses are what get taken.** USFWS 12-month finding on Pacific walrus, 76 FR 7634 (2011), PDF: https://www.north-slope.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/76_fr_7634_walrus_finding.pdf — verified 2026-11-13.
  - "some of the walruses were in poor condition and polar bears were able to kill them relatively easily. At least 11 cases of polar bear predation on motherless calves"
  - "some polar bear routinely rush herds to cause a stampede, expecting that some calves will be left behind"
- **Adult walruses are hard to kill. Bears often hold back.** WWF Arctic Programme, *The Circle* 2.2017, "Walruses: going with the floes?": https://admin.arcticwwf.org/app/uploads/2022/04/29092902/circle_0217_walrus.pdf — verified 2026-11-13.
  - "an adult male walrus can more than match most polar bears with its size, strength, incredibly tough hide, and dangerous tusks"
  - "They can and do mortally injure polar bears so it is common to see bears showing deference to adult walrus."
  - "in open water the advantage goes clearly to the walrus"
- **Even young walruses take a long time to kill.** Polar Bears International (written with Ian Stirling), "Do Polar Bears Invent Tools?": https://polarbearsinternational.org/news-media/articles/do-polar-bears-use-tools — verified 2026-11-13.
  - "A subadult walrus may take hours to kill, even by an adult male polar bear"
- ADF&G (both profiles) lists polar bears among walrus predators and walrus in the polar bear diet. Verified.
- **Success rate:** I found no quantitative kill-success rate for polar bears attacking adult walruses. Calvert & Stirling 1990 (*Int. Conf. Bear Res. Manage.* 8:351–356) is the primary study, but I only saw it as cited by Derocher et al. Not fetched directly, so NOT verified.
- **Avoid each other?** Partly. Bears are drawn to walrus haulouts but tend to defer to adult walruses (WWF Circle quote above). Adult walruses usually flee into the water. "Usually avoid each other" is too strong. The accurate caveat is: "Bears usually leave healthy adult walruses alone."

### Proposed stat cards

1. **Walrus bull: 3,000 lb (1,360 kg)**. Sources: NOAA Circ. 32 ("3,000 pounds") and NatGeo ("up to 1.5 tons" = 1.5 × 2,000 = 3,000 lb). Arithmetic: 3,000 ÷ 2.2046 = 1,360.8 kg, rounded to 1,360 kg. (ADF&G gives "up to 2 tons" = 4,000 lb, which I'm treating as the upper extreme. 3,000 lb is the figure two sources agree on.)
2. **Polar bear male: up to 1,700 lb (770 kg)**. Sources: ADF&G ("in excess of 1700 lbs") and PBS ("550-1700 pounds"). Arithmetic: 1,700 ÷ 2.2046 = 771.1 kg, rounded to 770 kg. WWF-UK's "up to 800kg" is consistent.
3. **Walrus tusks: up to 39 in (1 m)**. Sources: SeaWorld ("100 cm (39 in.)"), NOAA Circ. 32 ("39-1/2 inches"), NatGeo ("about three feet"). Arithmetic: 100 cm ÷ 2.54 = 39.4 in.
   - Alternative card: **Polar bear sprint: 25 mph (40 km/h)**. Sources: Brookfield Zoo and PBS. Arithmetic: 25 × 1.609 = 40.2 km/h.

### Proposed reveal (≤110 chars)

"In most real encounters, polar bears leave healthy adult walruses alone and go after calves or weak ones." (105 chars)
Caveat line: "Bears usually leave healthy adult walruses alone."

### Encounter behaviour (real world)

- **Jaguars eat anacondas (documented, but reported rarely).** Leite Pitman R. et al. 2019, "Feeding on giants: consumption of anacondas by jaguars", *CATnews* (IUCN/SSC Cat Specialist Group, ISSN 1027-2992). Abstract verified on the lead author's Duke Academia profile page: https://duke.academia.edu/RenataLeitePitman — verified 2026-11-13.
  - "very little is reported about behavioural interactions between jaguars and these snake species"
  - "The first was a green anaconda that was consumed by a jaguar in Madre de Dios, Peru. The second was a yellow anaconda being disputed by a mother jaguar and its cub."
  - The "5.2 m" length of the Peru anaconda appears only in an Academia AI summary. The full text (https://www.academia.edu/41350215/) returned 403. NOT verified, so don't use it.
- **Zoo fact sheet lists anaconda as jaguar prey.** SDZWA Library, Jaguar Diet & Feeding: https://ielc.libguides.com/sdzg/factsheets/jaguar/diet — "Eat caiman (alligator), fish, turtles, iguana, anaconda, and birds". Verified 2026-11-13.
- **Anaconda eating a jaguar: no documented case found.**
  - Thomas & Allain 2021, "A review of prey taken by anacondas", *Reptiles & Amphibians* 28:329–334, doi:10.17161/randa.v28i2.15504: https://journals.ku.edu/reptilesandamphibians/article/download/15504/14058/36316 — verified 2026-11-13. The prey table's Carnivora rows list domestic dog, crab-eating fox, domestic cat, Neotropical otter and pampas fox. There is no jaguar (*Panthera onca*) entry.
  - Closest case: an anaconda killed and swallowed an adult female **puma**, not a jaguar. Cavalcanti et al. 2016, "Predation of an adult puma by an anaconda in southeastern Brazil", *CATnews* 63: https://procarnivoros.org.br/downloads/predation-of-an-adult-puma-by-an-anaconda-in-southeastern-brazil — verified 2026-11-13.
    - "they found a large anaconda, measuring 4.20 m and weighing 94 kg … Apparently, the puma had been swallowed by the anaconda."
    - "To our knowledge, this is the first record of an anaconda preying on a top predator like the puma."
    - The anaconda had severe claw/bite injuries and died. The puma weighed 42 kg.
  - Popular claims that anacondas eat jaguars appear only on blogs. They are unconfirmed, so say "no confirmed record found".
- **Avoid each other?** No source says this directly. They share habitat (Leite Pitman 2019: "potential for spatial and dietary overlap"), and encounters are reported rarely. Suggested caveat: "Real clashes are rarely recorded."

### Proposed stat cards

1. **Green anaconda: up to 550 lb (250 kg)**. Sources: Smithsonian National Zoo, NatGeo. Arithmetic: 550 ÷ 2.2046 = 249.5 kg, which matches the Zoo's stated 250 kg.
2. **Green anaconda: up to 30 ft (9 m)**. Sources: National Zoo ("30 feet (9 meters)"), NatGeo ("more than 29 feet"). Arithmetic: 30 × 0.3048 = 9.1 m.
3. **Jaguar: up to 250 lb (113 kg)**. Sources: NatGeo ("100 to 250 pounds"), SDZWA ("57-113 kg (126-249 lb)"). Arithmetic: 250 ÷ 2.2046 = 113.4 kg.
   - Alternative bite card: **Jaguar bite: ~1,000 N (~228 lbf) at the canines, a top-ranked big-cat bite for its size**. Source: Wroe et al. 2005 (BFQ 137 vs tiger 127, lion 112 in the same table; clouded leopard also 137). Arithmetic: 1,014 N × 0.2248 = 227.9 lbf. Label it "estimated" because the figures come from skulls. Do NOT write "1,500 psi".

### Proposed reveal (≤110 chars)

"Jaguars are on record eating anacondas. A confirmed case of an anaconda eating a jaguar? None found." (100 chars)

### Encounter behaviour (real world)

- **Honey badgers really do hunt cobras (in Africa).** Begg et al. 2003, *J. Zool.*: https://www.honeybadger.com/pdfs/diet.pdf — verified 2026-11-13.
  - "mole snake Pseudaspis cana (21.4%), puff adder Bitis arientans (12.5%) and Cape cobra Naja nivea (10.5%), each contributed >10%"
  - The snakes in question are Cape cobras, not king cobras. Cape cobras live in southern Africa.
- **Bitten and recovered (puff adder, not cobra).** Begg & Begg, honeybadger.com (the researchers' own site): https://www.honeybadger.com — verified 2026-11-13.
  - "A honey badger bitten on the face by the highly cytotoxic puff adder showed signs of severe pain but recovered fully within five hours"
- **Not invincible.** The honeybadger.com FAQ (https://www.honeybadger.com/faq.html) says an African rock python killed a honey badger on one occasion. Verified via fetch summary 2026-11-13, but the exact wording wasn't confirmed verbatim, so treat it as a paraphrase.
- **Do snake bites kill honey badgers?** I found no reputable source documenting a honey badger killed by a venomous snakebite. Drabeck 2015 covers only resistance to α-neurotoxins, and IFAW says "not immune". The claim "bites can kill them" is NOT verified. Say "not immune" instead.
- **Range overlap.**
  - King cobra: "eastern Pakistan, northern and eastern India, the Andaman Islands, southern China…" (NatGeo king cobra page, verified 2026-11-13).
  - Honey badger: present in India, though rare. Frontiers 2020 says "In India, the species is relatively rare". JoTT 2024 (https://threatenedtaxa.org/index.php/JoTT/article/view/9209, verified 2026-11-13) reports "the first known camera-trap records of this species in Tarai East Forest Division (TEFD), Uttarakhand, India".
  - Inference (not a quoted claim): the two ranges likely overlap in parts of India. I found no published record of a honey badger and a king cobra meeting.
- **Documented honey badger vs king cobra fights: none found.** One Discover Wildlife (BBC Wildlife) article says honey badgers eat king cobras "in southwest Asia, where the species overlap". That is unsourced and geographically doubtful (king cobras don't live in southwest Asia). NOT used.
- **Avoid each other?** No source. Encounters are probably rare because both species are uncommon where they co-occur. That is an inference, so don't state it as fact. Suggested caveat: "They rarely, if ever, meet in the wild."

### Proposed stat cards

1. **King cobra: up to 18 ft (5.4 m)**. Sources: Smithsonian National Zoo, NatGeo. Arithmetic: 18 × 0.3048 = 5.49 m; the Zoo states 5.4 m.
2. **Honey badger: up to 30 lb (14 kg)**. Sources: NatGeo ("13 to 30 pounds"), Frontiers 2020 ("6–14 kg"). Arithmetic: 14 × 2.2046 = 30.9 lb, which matches 30 lb.
3. **Honey badger skin: about ¼ in (6 mm) thick**. Source: IFAW ("6 to 8 millimeters (0.25 to 0.31 inches)"). Arithmetic: 6 mm ÷ 25.4 = 0.24 in. SINGLE SOURCE, so mark it as such or swap in the alternative below.
   - Alternative card: **King cobra venom: up to 0.2 fl oz (~6 mL) per bite**. Source: NatGeo. Arithmetic: 0.2 × 29.57 = 5.9 mL. Corroborated in a different unit by Tan et al. 2022 (">1 gram (dry weight)").

### Proposed reveal (≤110 chars)

"Honey badgers really do hunt cobras, but no real fight with a king cobra has been scientifically documented." (108 chars)
Caveat line: "They rarely, if ever, meet in the wild."
